Every question in the tool asks about a system or process an outside assessor could verify by watching one case go from first call to aftercare. None asks about intent. Each answer ladder follows the same five-step logic: nothing in place, informal or manual, defined at one location, managed across the network, then optimizing and feeding other capabilities. A respondent can place themselves by asking "is this fully true today?" without guessing which answer is "right." Every question also has to separate homes within deathcare. Almost every home has a phone and some web presence, so we avoided questions every home would answer the same way.

**Case & Records Infrastructure.** The deathcare case file is legally consequential: it holds permits, the death certificate and often a preneed contract sold years earlier. We therefore anchored the dimension on three moments where record quality is exposed. First is how the case file is created. Second is how the death certificate reaches the state, which moves from in-person filing to fax, to manual EDRS entry, to EDRS integrated with case management. Third is whether an old preneed contract can be found when the death occurs, including one sold at another location. That last question separates single-location homes from real networks. A fourth question on record ownership tests governance rather than software.

**Family Digital Engagement.** A website is not diagnostic in this industry; what a family can do on it is. The website question climbs from a phone listing to completing the whole arrangement and a preneed purchase online, the model direct-to-consumer providers like Tulip Cremation use. Livestreaming became standard after 2020 but is delivered very differently: ad hoc, on request, by default, or archived into a tribute page. Payment and aftercare round out the family journey. Aftercare matters because the relationship after the service drives referrals and preneed sales.

**Operational Automation.** We picked the three back-office workflows unique to funeral service: scheduling staff and removal vehicles, casket and urn inventory, and life-insurance assignments. Assignments are a strong discriminator because some homes still mail paper forms to carriers while large operators track them on dashboards. The fourth question asks whether automation opportunities are found systematically, which separates a home that bought one tool from one that keeps improving.

**Predictive & AI Deployment.** Forecasting case volume is the most realistic first AI use in deathcare, so it anchors the dimension. Because families are grieving, we added a question found in no generic model: whether family-facing AI output is reviewed for tone and sensitivity, and whether that review decides if the tool stays. The other two questions separate a monitored production tool from an unmonitored website chatbot, and a strategy-led roadmap from buying whatever a vendor pitches.

**Organizational Change Capacity.** Funeral directors are the product; families trust a person, not a system. The questions therefore ask whether directors are involved in technology decisions, how they are trained, and whether a dedicated change role exists. The strongest discriminator is whether the old paper process is actually retired. Keeping paper files "just in case" is the most common sign of a stalled rollout in this industry, and anyone can see it.

**Testing.** We simulated a Stage 2 home (scores 1.5–2.25) and a Stage 4 network (3.75–4.25). They produced visibly different radar shapes and different profiles, *The Traditional Home* versus *Digital-Native Network*, and each gap pattern in the Interpretation Guide was reproduced on purpose.
